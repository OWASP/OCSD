# Module 9 – Secure Communications

## 9.1 Transport protection and TLS termination

- Confidentiality, integrity, and peer authentication on a Transport Layer Security (TLS) connection, and the risks of interception, tampering, and credential or session theft on an unprotected connection
- Where TLS protection ends at a browser, proxy, or service, and the need for separate protection and peer verification on onward connections, including internal connections
- The responsibilities that remain with endpoints, including protecting data after decryption and enforcing application access rules
- The use of maintained TLS libraries, frameworks, or services and the need for secure settings at each endpoint

## 9.2 TLS versions, cipher suites, and forward secrecy

- TLS 1.3 as the preferred version, TLS 1.2 with secure settings where compatibility requires it, and rejection of older TLS and Secure Sockets Layer (SSL) versions
- Negotiation of a mutually supported version and cipher suite, the purpose of downgrade prevention, and connection failure when peers share no acceptable settings
- The difference between negotiating an allowed version and weakening the TLS settings on a retry or falling back to unencrypted transport
- Encryption and tampering detection in the standard TLS 1.3 suites, and the choice of TLS 1.2 suites that provide those protections, authenticate the server, and use temporary keys to establish a shared secret
- How the TLS 1.2 cipher suite specifies the key exchange method and how the TLS 1.3 handshake selects it separately
- Forward secrecy as protection of past traffic after the server's long-term private key is stolen, and why older suites that use that key to recover the connection secret lack this protection
- How fresh temporary keys affect forward secrecy for traffic sent after a resumed TLS 1.3 handshake

## 9.3 Certificate trust and lifecycle

- Verification of the chain to a trusted certificate authority, the expected service name, and the current validity period
- Public trust for browser-facing services and explicitly configured private trust for internal services where appropriate
- Responses to untrusted issuers, mismatched names, and certificates outside their validity period that stop the request and preserve verification
- Certificate scope, protection of private keys, and renewal and deployment of certificates before expiration
- Replacement of compromised private keys and revocation of the associated certificates

## 9.4 Mutual TLS and service identity

- Which peer the client verifies in server-authenticated TLS and how mutual TLS (mTLS) adds verification of the client's certificate and identity
- The role of verified service identities in application access decisions, and the difference between establishing identity and granting permission

## 9.5 HTTPS in browsers

- HTTPS as Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) protected by TLS, and the exposure of a request sent over HTTP before a redirect takes effect
- HTTP Strict Transport Security (HSTS) as a browser policy that upgrades requests to HTTPS and prevents bypass of certificate errors for a known HSTS host
- How the browser learns HSTS from a valid HTTPS response, the effect of `max-age` and `includeSubDomains`, and the first-connection behavior of preloaded and previously unknown hosts
- The risk of mixed content on an HTTPS page and the browser's role in upgrading or blocking insecure requests
- The purposes of the Content Security Policy (CSP) directives `upgrade-insecure-requests` and `connect-src` for resource upgrades and browser connection restrictions

## 9.6 Secure WebSockets

- The difference between `ws://` and `wss://` and the protection TLS provides to the WebSocket handshake and messages
- Browser origin checks when accepting a connection and the application's separate responsibilities for authentication, session validity, and authorization of actions
- Message validation and connection and message limits for long-lived connections
