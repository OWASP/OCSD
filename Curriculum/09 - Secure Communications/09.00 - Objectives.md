# Objectives for Module 9 - Secure Communications


A successful candidate can:

- Explain why maintained frameworks, client libraries, and managed services are preferable to custom ("roll-your-own") cryptographic or transport-security implementations, while recognizing the continuing need for secure configuration.
- Explain how TLS provides confidentiality, integrity, and endpoint authentication, and recognize why it does not replace application authorization or protect data after it reaches the endpoint.
- Select appropriate secure transport protocols and TLS configurations for external and internal connections, recognizing recommended protocol versions, secure cipher characteristics, obsolete or weak options, and unsafe fallback to unencrypted communication.
- Recognize certificate-validation failures involving trust chains, hostname identity, or validity periods, and select responses that preserve verification of the intended endpoint rather than bypassing validation.
- Recognize certificate and private-key lifecycle requirements, including secure key handling, appropriate certificate scope, timely renewal, rotation, and replacement or revocation following private-key compromise.
- Explain the advantages of forward secrecy, and recognize the role of ephemeral key exchange.
- Recognize the purpose and limitations of certificate or public-key pinning in non-browser apps, and select safe responses to pin mismatches
- Distinguish the purposes and limitations of HTTPS redirection and HSTS, including the meaning of `max-age` and `includeSubDomains`, the role of preloading, and first-connection limitations.
- Identify mixed-content risks and recognize how Content Security Policy (CSP), particularly upgrade-insecure-requests and connect-src, can upgrade insecure resource requests and restrict browser connection destinations without replacing TLS, HSTS, or server-side authorization.
- Identify gaps in transport protection across client, proxy, and service-to-service connections, including connections after TLS termination, and select appropriate encryption and peer-verification controls.
- Recognize WebSocket risks that remain when `wss://` is used, and select appropriate origin, authentication, session, authorization, message-validation, rate-limiting, or resource-control protections.
- Distinguish server-authenticated TLS from mutual TLS (mTLS), including which peer identities are verified, how verified certificate identities can inform application access decisions, and why authentication alone does not grant authorization.

- Explain how the `Secure` cookie attribute affects cookie transmission, distinguish it from `HttpOnly` and `SameSite`, and recognize its purpose and limitations.

## Competencies

Candidates can distinguish secure from insecure communication choices in ordinary web development scenarios and select an appropriate explanation, mitigation, or expected behavior.
For example, given a web application behind a reverse proxy that calls an external API, they can identify an unprotected connection or an invalid certificate and choose a response that maintains security.
