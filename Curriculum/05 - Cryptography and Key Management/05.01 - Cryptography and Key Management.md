# Module 5 – Cryptography and Key Management

## 5.1 Security properties and data transformations

- Confidentiality as protection from unauthorized disclosure, integrity as protection from undetected changes, and authenticity as assurance about the source of data
- Encryption as a reversible transformation from readable data (plaintext) to encrypted data (ciphertext) using a key
- Cryptographic hashing as a one-way transformation to a fixed-length value called a digest
- Hash collisions, where different inputs produce the same digest, and why Message Digest 5 (MD5) and Secure Hash Algorithm 1 (SHA-1) are unsuitable for new designs that rely on collision resistance
- SHA-256 from the Secure Hash Algorithm 2 (SHA-2) family as an example of a general-purpose cryptographic hash and the need for a trusted expected digest when checking content
- Encoding, such as Base64, as a reversible change in representation that requires no secret and provides no confidentiality
- Purpose-built password hashing, such as Argon2id, and the roles of a unique salt added to each hash computation and a deliberately expensive algorithm in resisting offline guessing

## 5.2 Encryption and authenticated encryption

- Symmetric encryption with a shared secret key and public-key encryption with a public key and its matching private key
- The use of public-key mechanisms to establish or protect a shared key and symmetric encryption to protect application data
- Authenticated encryption with associated data (AEAD) as protection for plaintext confidentiality and for the integrity of ciphertext and optional unencrypted metadata
- Advanced Encryption Standard in Galois/Counter Mode (AES-GCM) and ChaCha20-Poly1305 as examples of authenticated encryption
- The need for a unique nonce for every encryption with a given key in AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305, and why requirements for these starting values depend on the algorithm and mode
- Recognition of unsafe choices, including Electronic Codebook (ECB) mode, encryption that leaves ciphertext open to tampering, and custom cryptographic constructions
- Maintained libraries and services that provide standard constructions, and rejection of ciphertext when authentication fails

## 5.3 Message authentication and digital signatures

- A message authentication code (MAC) as a tag that can show a message came from a holder of the shared key and retained its integrity, and a hash-based message authentication code (HMAC) as a standard example
- The ability of a MAC verifier with the shared key to generate a valid MAC
- Digital signatures generated with a private key and verified with the corresponding public key
- The need to obtain the verification key and allowed algorithm from a trusted configuration, and to verify the protected data before relying on it
- What successful verification establishes about the protected data and its key, and why authorization of the sender remains a separate decision
- Replay of an authentic message and the need for a separate check that the message is fresh or has not already been used

## 5.4 Secure randomness and resistance to guessing

- Brute-force guessing as a search through possible keys, and how key size and unpredictability affect the work required
- Unpredictability for cryptographic keys and secret tokens, and the consequences of using predictable values
- Cryptographically secure random number generators (CSPRNGs) provided by operating systems, libraries, or frameworks, and their distinction from general-purpose random generators
- The role of entropy as a measure of how unpredictable a source is, and why seeding a generator from predictable inputs is unsafe
- The different roles of secret random values, password salts, and encryption nonces

## 5.5 Key agreement and public-key trust

- Key agreement as a way for two parties to derive a shared secret without sending the secret between them
- The role of authentication in establishing who shares the secret, and the use of derived session keys for symmetric encryption
- X.509 certificates as signed bindings between public keys and identities, and the role of a certificate chain in establishing trust in that binding
- Forward secrecy as protection for past sessions after compromise of a long-term key when key agreement used temporary keys and the session secrets remain protected

## 5.6 Key management

- Key generation with sufficient strength and secure randomness, and separation of keys by purpose and environment
- Secure storage and distribution of secret and private keys, and trusted distribution of public keys
- Key management services, protected keystores, and hardware security modules as options for limiting access to key material and operations
- Least privilege for key access, separation of keys from the data they protect, and auditing key use and lifecycle events
- Key ownership, versioning, and the factors that determine how long a key can be used, including purpose, use volume, and risk
- Rotation for new operations and controlled retention for decrypting data or verifying messages already protected
- The difference between scheduled rotation and the response to suspected compromise, including restricting use, replacing keys, and assessing affected data and trust
- Recovery of data and backups that depend on older keys, and the consequences of destroying a key before those dependencies expire or are migrated
